# UTOPIA UNDONE: FROM THE KIBBUTZ TO GAZA
## Hebrew Fiction and the End of Redemption

### A Book Proposal

**Author:** Itay Eisinger, PhD
**Date:** December 2025

---

## BRIEF DESCRIPTION

In 2013, Israeli novelist Yigal Sarna published *2023*, depicting mass emigration, economic collapse, and social fracture. A decade later, his fictional projections had become documentary reality: international relocation firms reported unprecedented inquiries from Israeli professionals, economic indicators showed concerning trends, and social cohesion appeared increasingly strained. In October 2023, as Israeli soldiers massed at Gaza's borders, a Jerusalem Post columnist observed: "We are living inside Yishai Sarid's novel." Eight years earlier, Sarid's *The Third* had imagined a theocratic Israel where Temple priests process sacrifice permits through government databases while secular Jews flee abroad. Fiction had become field notes.

This uncanny accuracy reveals Hebrew dystopian literature's distinctive function as **cultural seismograph**—detecting societal tremors years before they register in political discourse.

For over a century, Zionism advanced a redemption narrative: from precarious exile through return to the Land of Israel toward messianic fulfillment. Modern Hebrew literature carried this story forward, particularly after 1948, when fiction became the arena where redemptive promises were tested against lived reality. Yet since 1984—Orwell's year—the dominant mode of Hebrew fiction has become dystopian, marking what I argue is a fundamental generic shift that diagnoses the **state-as-antagonist** before political discourse can articulate it.

The dystopian turn began precisely in 1984 when Amos Kenan's *The Road to Ein Harod* and Benjamin Tammuz's *Jeremiah's Inn* simultaneously positioned Israel's state apparatus as primary threat rather than protective shelter. These foundational texts introduced what I term **double temporality**: biblical destructions (586 BCE, 70 CE) bleeding into administrative present, creating anticipatory trauma where future catastrophe determines current action. Literary form itself becomes diagnostic instrument—fragmented narratives mirror social dissolution, temporal loops reveal historical compulsion, procedural prose tracks bureaucratic normalization of emergency.

*Utopia Undone* analyzes twenty-two Hebrew novels published between 1984 and 2024, reading formal innovations as symptoms of political transformation. Drawing on trauma theory and political theology, I demonstrate how these texts develop distinctive analytical concepts: **pre-PTSD** (collective trauma from catastrophes not yet occurred but already remembered), **administrative realism** (catastrophe normalized through procedure rather than declared through emergency), and **Gaza as structure** (siege consciousness as general governmental technique). All translations are my own, preserving linguistic innovations that mark dystopian consciousness.

The journey spans from kibbutz to Gaza: from Ein Harod as pioneer settlement to Gaza as the space where democratic ideals meet their terminus. For the first time in Jewish textual tradition, the state itself becomes the enemy.

## THEORETICAL FRAMEWORK: FOUR DIAGNOSTIC CONCEPTS

### 1. Cultural Seismograph
Literature as early detection system for ideological shifts. These novels function as instruments detecting political tremors through narrative breakdown. When plots stall at checkpoints, when time fractures, when form itself destabilizes, literature registers governance mutations before they surface in political discourse.

### 2. State-as-Antagonist
The fundamental reversal: sovereign power as primary threat to population rather than protective shelter. This concept tracks how the state transforms from guarantor of safety to source of danger—not through dramatic coup but through administrative normalization.

### 3. Pre-PTSD (Anticipatory Trauma)
Collective trauma from catastrophes not yet occurred but already remembered. Forward-facing dread where anticipated disasters organize present life. Characters live in tomorrow's ruins while navigating today's bureaucracies.

### 4. Double Temporality
Biblical destructions structuring contemporary experience. Ancient and contemporary time collapse simultaneously—Kenan's time-travel warfare, Sarid's Temple bureaucracy, Burstein's prophets on Jerusalem's light rail reveal how states invoke eternal authority for temporal violence.

## CHAPTER OUTLINE

### PROLOGUE — ZERO LINE: Herzl's Garden (1902)
*Theodor Herzl, Altneuland* — **2,000 words**

The baseline is engineered harmony. *Altneuland* stages a technological garden where rational planning resolves conflict by design. This marks zero on the seismograph: calm produced through administrative foresight. Herzl's faith in expert systems supplies the template later texts will systematically dismantle. The instruments of coordination—databases, committees, expert reason—reappear as vectors of control. This prologue establishes the equilibrium against which all tremors will be measured.

---

### CHAPTER 1 — EARLY WARNING SYSTEMS (1984-1987)
*Amos Kenan, Benjamin Tammuz, Yitzhak Ben-Ner* — **12,000 words**

**Primary texts:** *The Road to Ein Harod* (1984), *Jeremiah's Inn* (1984), *The Angels Are Coming* (1987)

Kenan's *The Road to Ein Harod* registers the inaugural break. State-as-antagonist becomes narratable. Civil war appears not as fever dream but as plausible structure. Secular refugees flee religious militias through a Galilee that reads as Lebanon rather than homeland. The kibbutz persists only as rumor. The protagonist arrives at Ein Harod to find Israel utterly destroyed, returned to pre-settlement wilderness. Paradoxically, he declares: "Now, at last, I am happy." How do we read this ending? What does it mean that happiness arrives in annihilation?

Tammuz's *Jeremiah's Inn* meditates on prophetic fatigue: the state neither collapses nor redeems itself, leaving characters suspended in moral drift. Ben-Ner's *The Angels Are Coming* portrays society paralyzed by friction between religious myth and secular doubt, presciently diagnosing theocratic tendencies dominating later fiction.

Through analysis of linguistic transformation—Hebrew shifting from redemptive to catastrophic idiom—I demonstrate how literary form becomes methodological instrument for detecting Labor Zionism's ideological exhaustion before its electoral collapse.

---

### CHAPTER 2 — OSLO'S GROTESQUE (1992-2004)
*Orly Castel-Bloom* — **14,000 words**

**Primary texts:** *Dolly City* (1992), *Human Parts* (2002)

Castel-Bloom transforms satire into surgical allegory. The body becomes policy's landing zone. In *Dolly City*, Dr. Dolly adopts a starving baby and subjects him to obsessive medical experiments—grafting biblical maps onto his skin with a scalpel. The mother performs endless surgeries on her child to "protect" him—savage allegory for Israeli society's self-mutilation through security obsession.

*Human Parts* navigates Second Intifada violence as narrative structure mimics checkpointed geography. Form enacts Oslo's whiplash—ecstatic promise followed by violent recoil. Language fractures into shards mimicking a polity in pieces. The grotesque becomes methodological principle: apparent exaggeration reveals underlying truth, medical comedy becomes political horror, domestic intimacy becomes battlefield.

Castel-Bloom provides the grammar for later dystopias: protection produces injury, care becomes control. The clinic replaces the barricade; the ledger outlives the rally.

---

### CHAPTER 3 — LET IT BE MORNING (2004-2010)
*Sayed Kashua* — **13,000 words**

**Primary texts:** *Let It Be Morning* (2004), *Second Person Singular* (2010)

Kashua's intervention transforms Hebrew dystopian theory by revealing the genre's constitutive blindness: what Jewish-Israelis experience as future anxiety, Palestinian-Israelis inhabit as present reality. An Arab-Israeli journalist returns to his village to escape anti-Arab racism in Jerusalem. One morning, villagers wake to find Israeli army tanks surrounding the village. No exit. After weeks of siege, the encirclement lifts—because a "peace treaty" has transferred the village to a new Palestinian state, stripping Arab-Israeli citizenship.

Yet this isn't speculative fiction—it documents Palestinian everyday experience under occupation's bureaucratic apparatus. Kashua's genius forces Jewish-Israeli readers to experience their security system from the receiving end, revealing how protection for some necessitates imprisonment for others.

His Hebrew—language marking him as ethnic Other while serving as literary medium—operates through **split temporality**: existing simultaneously in majority's future fears and minority's present reality. *Second Person Singular* extends this analysis, depicting Palestinian-Israelis navigating impossible identities between integration and resistance. This chapter establishes dystopia as methodology of the oppressed rather than merely anxiety of the oppressor.

---

### CHAPTER 4 — FROM CRITIQUE TO CATASTROPHE (2012-2014)
*Yigal Sarna, Ilana Bernstein, Avivit Mishmari* — **11,000 words**

**Primary texts:** *2023* (2014), *Terminal* (2013), *The Old Man Lost His Mind* (2013)

The compressed period 2012-2014 witnessed unprecedented dystopian proliferation, marking genre's movement from literary margin to cultural mainstream. These breakthrough works establish **administrative realism** as dominant mode.

Sarna's *2023* captures the atmosphere of rational departure. Post-apocalyptic Tel Aviv: empty streets, abandoned homes, books the only thing untouched. Father and daughter prepare papers to escape by boat. This reverses Zionist return narrative—depicting emigration as rational endpoint. When does leaving become logical? When does survival require departure from homeland?

Bernstein's *Terminal* reveals healthcare weaponized against citizens. Mishmari's *The Old Man Lost His Mind* brings dystopia home through generational trauma and care work. After an elderly politician incites secular Israelis to violently resist religious extremism, civil war erupts. The novel zooms inward—into domestic spaces where gendered care labor absorbs perpetual emergency.

The state no longer promises security but manages insecurity; democracy doesn't prevent authoritarianism but proceduralizes it through administrative routine.

---

### CHAPTER 5 — TEMPLE-STATE (2015-2016)
*Yishai Sarid, Dror Burstein* — **13,000 words**

**Primary texts:** *The Third* (2015), *Muck* (2016)

Contemporary theocratic dystopias reveal messianic fulfillment arriving not through divine intervention but bureaucratic procedure.

Sarid's *The Third* imagines Israel after successful Temple reconstruction. The shocking innovation: theocracy operates through administrative workflow. Temple priests process purity applications through government databases. Committees determine sacrifice protocols, algorithms calculate ritual purity, databases track genealogical legitimacy—the sacred becomes another government department. The king demands ultimate sacrifice (his own son), not through miraculous vision but through administrative logic.

Burstein's *Muck* thickens the medium until governance feels like texture. The novel conflates biblical prophecy (7th-century BCE Jeremiah) with present-day Israel seamlessly—cell phones, light rail, First Temple, Babylonian tanks all occupying same narrative space. Bodies move through muck until control persists not through decree but through friction, tempo, viscosity.

Both novels expose what Gershom Scholem called the "apocalyptic sting" inherent in messianic politics: redemptive fulfillment becomes source of destruction, Temple reconstruction institutionalizes rather than transcends sacrifice, return perpetuates rather than heals exile.

---

### CHAPTER 6 — FACING GAZA (2017-2024)
*Assaf Gavron, Carmi Gillon, contemporary responses* — **12,000 words**

**Primary texts:** *The Hilltop* (2017), *A Simple Story* (2019), post-October 7 responses

The final phase examines Gaza's transformation from geographic periphery to dystopian paradigm, revealing siege not as exceptional measure but as generalizable governmental technique.

**Gaza as structure** means the techniques perfected "there" script life "here." The border becomes mirror. What Kenan's rupture initiated, what Castel-Bloom's grotesque registered, what Kashua diagnosed as permanent for some, what the Temple-state novels proceduralized—all roads lead to Gaza as organizing principle of perpetual emergency.

Gavron's *The Hilltop* shows settlement expansion as dystopian form. Gillon's *A Simple Story* reveals security apparatus consuming democratic institutions. The seismograph no longer distinguishes between crisis and routine because they have merged.

This is secular prophecy's final form: not warning of future collapse but mapping present structure. The exception has become the rule, the periphery has become the center, the temporary has become permanent.

### EPILOGUE — AFTER THE TREMOR
**2,500 words**

Hebrew literature maintains prophetic function without divine authority. The seismograph continues recording even as homeland becomes uninhabitable. What emerges: ethics of aftermath, responsibility without triumph. The journey from kibbutz to Gaza is complete—from socialist paradise through bureaucratic normalization to militarized siege as permanent governmental condition.

## SIGNIFICANCE AND CONTRIBUTION

This book fills a critical gap in Hebrew literary studies, dystopian studies, and political theology:

**For Hebrew literature scholars:** First comprehensive periodization demonstrating how political ruptures generate literary innovations. Makes visible a tradition fragmented across isolated readings.

**For comparative literature scholars:** Repositions non-Anglophone dystopian traditions as central, not peripheral. Demonstrates Hebrew/Jewish political-theological innovation reshaping genre conventions.

**For political theologians:** Shows how secular literature inherits and transforms prophetic modes without religious authority. Develops "secular prophecy" and "procedural theocracy" as analytical tools.

**For trauma studies scholars:** Offers new vocabulary for anticipatory violence and administrative harm through pre-PTSD and double temporality concepts.

**Contemporary relevance:** Hebrew dystopian literature provides essential vocabulary for understanding how democracies transform into administrative authoritarianism, how populations adapt to their own imprisonment through security discourse, and how literature serves as diagnostic instrument for political transformation—offering critical resources for understanding democratic crisis worldwide.
